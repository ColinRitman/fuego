Read the mint path for the first time and it looks like a conjuring trick. A user
destroys real XFG. In exchange the chain creates HEAT, a unit that targets
purchasing power rather than any reserve. Nothing was deposited. No vault was
credited. The coin that paid for the new unit does not exist any more.

A careful reader should distrust that, and a capable agent reading the source will
flag it within minutes. This page exists because the distrust is correct as an
instinct and wrong as a conclusion, and because the difference between the two rests
on one rule applied without exception.

<Note>
**The invariant: no unit of value may support two claims.**

Everything below is this rule applied to a specific mechanism. Where a mechanism
satisfies it, the mechanism is sound however strange it looks. Where a mechanism
violates it, no amount of design around the edges rescues it, and the honest move is
to delete the mechanism. One has been deleted for exactly this reason (see
[the reserve fund](#06-the-reserve-fund-is-a-budget-not-a-vault)).
</Note>

The rule is deliberately narrow. It does not say the chain holds reserves against
HEAT, because it does not. It does not say HEAT is redeemable, because it is not. It
says that value already promised to someone cannot be promised again, and that every
unit of HEAT traces to value that genuinely left somebody's control.

## 01 Destruction is a transfer, not a disappearance

**Objection.** The minter burns 1,000 XFG and receives 100 HEAT. The 1,000 XFG is
gone. Whatever supported those 100 HEAT at the instant of creation no longer exists,
so the HEAT is backed by nothing from its first block.

**Answer.** XFG has a hard cap. Removing 1,000 units from a fixed supply raises the
claim every remaining holder has on the whole. The value did not evaporate; it moved
to everyone still holding XFG. The minter traded a claim denominated in XFG for one
denominated in a consumer basket, and the chain recorded both sides.

Conservation at the mint is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is enforced rather than
assumed. Every sum in the validator runs over amounts the transaction author chose,
so each one is checked for wrap before it is used:

```cpp
// Every sum below is over amounts the transaction author chose, so each one
// must be checked: a wrapping sum turns an unbacked mint into a "valid" one.
bool addChecked(uint64_t& sum, uint64_t value) {
  if (sum > std::numeric_limits<uint64_t>::max() - value) return false;
  sum += value;
  return true;
}
```

The mint is rejected unless HEAT out is at or below what the burned XFG buys at the
oracle price. The price multiply is done in 128 bits so a large burn cannot overflow
into a small number, and `TransactionInputCommitmentTransfer` is refused as a burn
source outright, because counting it without valuing it would let a transaction claim
a burn it never made.

## 02 The mint premium is a haircut inside the burn

**Objection.** The protocol withholds 1% of every burn. That is a toll on the one
action the system most needs people to take, and it funds the protocol's own trading
position out of users' pockets.

**Answer.** The premium is taken out of the minter's entitlement, not added on top of
it. Burn 1,000 XFG and 100 HEAT comes into existence either way. The minter receives
99 and the protocol's liquidity position receives 1. It is a transfer between two
recipients of the same issuance, so supply is identical with the premium and without
it.

```text
premiumXfg   = xfgBurned × 100 / 10000        // 1%, withheld, never destroyed
userCap      = heat(xfgBurned) − heat(premiumXfg)
HEAT created = userCap + heat(premiumXfg)
             = heat(xfgBurned)                 // identical with or without the premium
```

The premium does one more thing that is easy to miss and hard to replicate another
way. A liquidity position needs two legs at the pool's ratio. The premium's HEAT leg
is defined as whatever its XFG leg buys at the current price, so the two legs arrive
already paired, with no conversion trade and no price impact. At the launch ratio of
ten XFG per HEAT, a 10 XFG leg pairs with a 1 HEAT leg, which is exactly the pool
ratio. The pairing is a consequence of the definition rather than a calibration that
could drift.

This is also why the premium cannot simply be raised to fund the position faster.
Taxing the mint harder suppresses minting, which shrinks the base the tax draws from.
The position is instead fed from trading fees, which scale with use rather than with
friction.

> `src/CryptoNoteConfig.h` — `HEAT_MINT_PREMIUM_BPS = 100`, `heatMintPremiumXfg`.
> `bankedBurn` excludes the premium precisely because it is not destroyed.

## 03 The Eternal Flame spends a burn twice, and that is permitted

Here is the part most summaries leave out, stated plainly before it is defended. Half
of every burn is credited to the Eternal Flame, and the Eternal Flame **raises the
block reward**. Burned XFG comes back as miner emission. So HEAT issuance outruns
permanent XFG retirement by roughly two to one, by design.

```cpp
Osavvirsak  = alreadyGeneratedCoins − eternalFlame;   // coins in circulation
if (Osavvirsak > m_moneySupply) return false;         // hard ceiling, enforced
baseReward  = (m_moneySupply − Osavvirsak) >> emissionSpeedFactor;
```

Two properties make this survivable, and both are checkable in the four lines above.

**It redistributes a capped asset.** The re-emission is bounded by `MONEY_SUPPLY`,
with an explicit guard that fails the block if the bound is crossed. XFG beyond
8,000,008.8 cannot be created by this path or any other. The Flame pulls emission
forward inside a ceiling that was always committed. It does not issue a new asset.

**It buys an expense, not backing.** The proceeds pay for hashpower, which is
consumed. Nobody holds a receipt saying the Flame owes them. Paying for a service
twice makes the payer poorer. Backing two liabilities with one asset makes the issuer
insolvent, and only the second failure compounds.

This is the reasoning that forbids the symmetrical-looking move elsewhere. A burn
tally may fund the security budget, which is an expense under a cap. The same tally
may not fund a redemption promise, which is a liability with no cap. The asymmetry is
not a convenience; it is the invariant deciding two cases differently because they are
different cases.

> `MONEY_SUPPLY = 80000088000008`

## 04 Protocol-minted HEAT is inventory, and its backing arrives at the sale

The strongest objection in the design, and the one that took longest to answer
honestly.

**Objection.** The protocol mints HEAT it did not pay for and places it in its own
market-making position. Every other participant had to burn XFG to obtain HEAT. This
is seigniorage, and seigniorage spent into a thin market is how algorithmic pegs fail.

**Answer.** Minted HEAT enters as pool inventory, not as circulating supply. The only
route out of the pool is a trade, and that trade pays real XFG in. At the moment the
unit reaches a holder, the pool has been paid for it. Backing is not absent; it is
contemporaneous with the sale rather than prior to it.

That argument holds only while four properties hold with it. Each is a constraint on
the code, not a statement of intent, and the loss of any one of them collapses the
position back into plain seigniorage.

| Property | Why it is load-bearing | Enforced by |
|---|---|---|
| **Bounded** | Unbounded issuance is a spiral; a ceiling is a policy | `PROTOCOL_LP_SHARE_CAP_PCT = 50` |
| **Non-withdrawable** | No key may extract it, so no path exists to circulation without a trade | no spend path to protocol shares |
| **Never yield** | Paying interest from it would convert inventory into a distribution | yield drawn from fees only |
| **Disclosed** | Counted in supply at the instant of mint, so no reader is surprised later | `m_heatSupply` credited |

The ceiling is denominated in liquidity-pool shares rather than in depth or price.
That choice matters more than the number. A share ratio cannot be moved by pushing the
market around, because it is not a price. The only way to reopen the protocol's room to
add liquidity is for somebody else to add real liquidity first, which dilutes the
protocol's share. The incentive points toward the outcome the cap exists to protect.

The genesis pool is tracked separately from the protocol's own position, so the ceiling
starts near zero with room to grow into rather than pinned at the limit on the first
block.

## 05 DIGM needs no reserve because its peg is a constant

DIGM is the outward-facing unit, built to travel to markets where XFG has no wrapped
representation and HEAT does not go. A stablecoin backed by a flatcoin invites the
obvious complaint that the backing recurses forever.

It does not recurse, for a reason that is one conditional in the validator. DIGM
cannot be minted unless HEAT inputs cover the peg:

```cpp
requiredHeat = digmOutputs × DIGM_PEG_HEAT_ATOMIC / COIN;
if (heatInputs < requiredHeat + fee) return false;
```

Every DIGM in existence locked real HEAT at issuance, and that HEAT already satisfied
every test in section 01. Because `DIGM_PEG_HEAT` is a fixed constant rather than a
market rate, the round trip is exactly supply-neutral: minting consumes 0.1 HEAT per
DIGM and redemption returns 0.1 HEAT per DIGM, the same number in both directions,
with no drift to accumulate. DIGM is the easiest unit in the system to reason about,
not the hardest.

The entire first-generation float is small enough to state in one line: a 10,000 DIGM
cap against a 0.1 HEAT peg is 1,000 HEAT of locked backing in total.

<Warning>
The risk in DIGM is not collateral. It is the bridge. On-chain a DIGM commitment
cannot be spent twice. Off-chain, whoever operates the bridge can issue claims against
nothing, and no amount of on-chain collateral discipline constrains that. Audit effort
belongs there, and the bridge implementation is currently a stub.
</Warning>

> `DIGM_PEG_HEAT = 1000000` (0.1 HEAT); `DigmBridgeEngine.h` is unimplemented

## 06 The reserve fund is a budget, not a vault

Half of every burn is tallied to a fund called the SWF. An earlier design converted
that tally into a HEAT balance and offered it as collateral for DIGM. That mechanism
has been removed, and the reasoning for removing it is the clearest single application
of the invariant in the system.

The tally counts XFG that the mint **destroyed**. That burn already paid for the
minter's HEAT. Converting the tally into a second balance issues a second claim
against value that is already spent. The counter records a transfer that has already
been made to every XFG holder, which is a receipt rather than a reserve. Nothing can
be spent out of it, because deflation is not an asset the chain holds.

What the counter can honestly do is cap. Used as a ceiling on how much HEAT the
protocol may mint for market-making, decremented as that room is consumed, it is a
budget. A budget that calls itself a budget asserts nothing false. The same number
offered as backing would have been a lie, and the difference between the two is
entirely in what is claimed for it.

The conversion was also the last place in the system that priced an epoch-boundary
operation at the live pool rate instead of a time-weighted average, which would have
let a single trade in the closing block set the rate. It was removed before it ever
ran.

> `src/CryptoNoteCore/Blockchain.cpp` — conversion removed with rationale in place;
> `m_swfBurnedXfgPendingHeat` retained as a statistic with no issuance authority

## 07 Why the oracle is the hard part

If HEAT's value were fixed by its index, no oracle would be needed and the mint would
price against the index directly. It does not. The mint prices against an eight-block
time-weighted average of the Hearth market, which is an admission in code that HEAT's
exchange value is market-determined.

That makes the oracle the component an attacker goes after, and it has accumulated
defences accordingly:

- Pricing uses the time-weighted average only. An earlier spot fallback let a swap
  earlier in the same block set the price a mint paid.
- The average excludes the mint's own block, so a transaction cannot contribute to the
  price it pays.
- The average reads the automated-market-maker spot, never the call-auction clearing
  price, so a pair of tiny crossed orders cannot manufacture an oracle reading.
- A per-block band caps how far the pool may move from its opening price, so the
  average cannot be dragged in a single block.
- Fewer than two samples returns no price at all, and the mint is refused rather than
  priced on thin data.

Each of those is a closed hole rather than a precaution, which is the useful thing to
know about them. The design has been attacked, on paper and in review, and the current
shape is what survived.

## 08 Where the fees go

Two revenue streams are implemented. The list is open by policy, and any new stream is
welcome provided it is value somebody actually paid the protocol.

| Stream | Rate | Split |
|---|---|---|
| Hearth taker fee | 1% of fill | 35 liquidity / 35 maker / 30 savers |
| Atomic swap fee | 1% + 1% | 80 savers / 20 bonus vault |

Three charges are deliberately **not** revenue, and saying so costs nothing while
hiding it would cost credibility. The mint premium is protocol inventory. The CD
creation fee is a development payment. Neither may fund interest. Nor may
burn-derived value, for the reason given in section 06.

Before CDs activate, the saver share of the Hearth fee has no claimants, so the whole
non-maker side funds liquidity instead of accruing to nobody. The schedule is set in
advance rather than discovered later.

## 09 What is not claimed

A document that only argues for soundness is an advertisement. These are the limits of
the claim, stated as flatly as the claims themselves.

**HEAT is not redeemable.** No mechanism exchanges HEAT for a fixed quantity of
anything. The only exit is a sale into the Hearth market at the prevailing price.
Anyone holding HEAT is exposed to that price.

**The cost index is a target, not a mechanism.** The FCI basket reference sets what one
unit is meant to represent and re-bases quarterly. It does not enforce that value. Its
on-chain reader has exactly one consumer, a status endpoint, and appears in no pricing
or redemption path. If supply grows faster than demand, HEAT trades below its target
and the index does not prevent it.

**Dilution is possible.** Protocol-minted inventory rests on a wager that added depth
attracts added demand. The wager is reasonable and may prove correct. It is not a
theorem. Treating the unit's definition as proof that supply cannot dilute is the
precise error that has broken every uncollateralised peg that broke, and the share
ceiling exists because this document declines to make it.

**Market depth is currently thin.** The seeded pool is a fraction of a percent of total
XFG supply. Thin depth is what makes oracle manipulation worth attempting and what
limits how much HEAT can be sold without slippage. Fee revenue compounds depth over
time; it cannot create it from a standing start. Depth has to be seeded by
contribution.

## 10 How to prove this document wrong

Soundness that cannot be tested is a slogan. Each item below is a specific thing to
check in the source. Any one of them failing falsifies the invariant, and the correct
response is to say so publicly rather than to adjust the argument.

1. **A key that can extract protocol liquidity.** Find a spend path that lets any
   private key withdraw the protocol's own pool position. The point-of-sale backing
   argument in section 04 depends entirely on its absence.
2. **A ceiling denominated in a price.** Check that the protocol-share cap is measured
   in pool shares. If it is ever rewritten against depth, spot or an average, it
   becomes manipulable by the same trades it is meant to constrain.
3. **Interest paid from a burn.** Trace any yield distribution back to its funding. If
   burn-derived value, the premium or the CD creation fee reaches an interest payment,
   the double-claim this document forbids has been made.
4. **Minted HEAT missing from supply.** Confirm that protocol-minted inventory is
   credited to total supply at the instant it is created. Inventory that does not
   appear in the figure is undisclosed issuance regardless of where it sits.
5. **An epoch operation priced at spot.** Search for a pool rate read at an epoch
   boundary without a time-weighted average. Epoch boundaries are deterministic, so a
   trade placed in the closing block sets that rate. Two such sites existed; both were
   found and corrected.
6. **Outward units issued without locked backing.** Verify that no DIGM can enter
   circulation without HEAT locked at the fixed peg. The bridge is where this breaks if
   it breaks.

## 11 Open at the time of writing

- HEAT issuance outruns permanent XFG retirement by roughly two to one, because the
  Eternal Flame returns half of each burn to emission. Defended in section 03, and
  still the largest single leak of value out of the system.
- The liquidity diversion ships disabled, keyed to an activation height that is unset.
  It changes nothing until a height ahead of the chain tip is chosen.
- The outward bridge is unimplemented. Section 05 names it as the system's real
  counterparty risk.
- Depth must be seeded by contribution before fee compounding has anything to compound.
